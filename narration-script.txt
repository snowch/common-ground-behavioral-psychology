c01s03 Chapter 1: A useful description can be checked.
Behaviour: an action defined for observation. Operational definition: a stated procedure for identifying or measuring something. Frequency: number of defined events in a period.

Describe what happened rather than starting with a character judgment. Define whether a return means placing the tool in its marked location before leaving.

A count also needs opportunities and a time period. Ten missed returns mean different things after ten uses and after a thousand.

c01s03b Chapter 1: Record the starting pattern.
Duration: how long a defined event lasts.

A baseline shows current variation rather than assuming a single event is typical. Record time, opportunities and relevant conditions.

A lower return rate could reflect access, instructions or consequences. The baseline describes a pattern; it does not prove which cause produced it.

c01s04 Chapter 1: The observer is part of the method.
Interobserver agreement: consistency between observers using the same definition. Reactivity: behaviour changes because it is observed. Confounding: another factor contributes to a pattern.

Two observers can check whether the definition is usable. Agreement does not by itself prove that the chosen measure captures the intended concept.

Monitoring can change behaviour and raise privacy concerns. Use fictional cases or your own low-stakes actions for course practice; do not covertly track others.

c01s05 Chapter 1: What does the evidence allow?
Behavioural research needs observable definitions, suitable comparisons and ethical procedures. A label such as lazy does not specify the conditions maintaining an action.

A baseline is descriptive evidence. Causation requires additional design and consideration of alternative changes.

c02s03 Chapter 2: Learning can concern predictive relations.
Unconditioned stimulus: stimulus eliciting a response without the relevant learning. Unconditioned response: response elicited by that stimulus.

In classical conditioning, experience with related events can change responding to a cue. An initially neutral cue can acquire predictive significance.

Food-related responses are a familiar example, but the precise response and prior learning must be specified. The kettle scene is an illustration, not a controlled conditioning experiment.

c02s03b Chapter 2: Name the cue and the learned response.
Conditioned stimulus: cue that acquires significance through learning. Conditioned response: response elicited by a learned cue. Acquisition: development of a learned response.

If the whistle reliably precedes drinks, it may become a conditioned cue and anticipation a learned response. A learned response need not copy the original response exactly.

Identify what was measured and what other cues were present. Simply noticing a sequence does not establish that this cue caused the response.

c02s04 Chapter 2: Prediction matters more than coincidence.
Contingency: how one event changes the probability of another. Contiguity: closeness in time or space. Prediction error: difference between expected and observed outcome.

Events close in time are not necessarily informative. A cue predicts an outcome better when the outcome is more likely with the cue than without it.

Researchers compare predictive relations and competing cues. A useful account of learning must explain what the cue adds beyond what is already expected.

c02s05 Chapter 2: What does the evidence allow?
Classical conditioning research shows that temporal pairing alone is not a complete explanation. Contingency, prior learning and context influence acquisition.

Anticipation in the fictional café could have several causes. A controlled comparison is needed to identify the whistle’s contribution.

c03s03 Chapter 3: Start with the action and its consequence.
Operant conditioning: learning involving action-consequence relations.

Reinforcement and punishment are defined by effects on future behaviour, not by whether a consequence sounds kind or harsh. An intended reward may fail to reinforce.

Specify the action, the consequence and the change over comparable opportunities. A single occurrence is not enough to establish a lasting effect.

c03s03b Chapter 3: Positive and negative mean addition and removal.
Positive punishment: adding a consequence that decreases behaviour.

Stopping the reminder tone after a return can be negative reinforcement if returns increase. Negative means removal, not bad.

Adding a welcome consequence can be positive reinforcement if the action increases. Adding an unpleasant event is punishment only if the target behaviour decreases.

c03s04 Chapter 3: Removal can also reduce behaviour.
Negative punishment: removing a consequence in a way that decreases behaviour. Functional definition: classification based on an observed effect. Unintended consequence: an effect beyond the intended target.

If removing access to a valued activity reduces a defined action, it meets a negative-punishment definition. The effect still needs evidence.

Classification is not ethical endorsement. Coercion, unfairness and unintended effects matter. Supportive access and skill teaching are often more appropriate than arranging aversive consequences.

c03s05 Chapter 3: What does the evidence allow?
Operant principles identify functional relations under specified conditions. Effects depend on timing, context, history and what the consequence means to the person.

A label does not establish efficacy or ethical acceptability. The course does not ask learners to impose punishments on others.

c04s03 Chapter 4: Shape the behaviour that matters.
Shaping: reinforcing successive approximations to a target. Successive approximation: a step closer to the target behaviour. Criterion: the defined standard for a response.

Choose a meaningful target, then recognize progress toward it using clear criteria. An approximation should move toward the intended skill rather than reward a superficial proxy.

For Jo, naming one accurate observation is a better first step than speaking quickly. Increase demands as the skill becomes reliable.

c04s03b Chapter 4: Break complex actions into teachable parts.
Task analysis: identifying components of a complex action. Chaining: learning linked components of a sequence. Prompt: a cue or assistance supporting a response.

A repair explanation can involve describing the observation, proposing alternatives and identifying a test. Prompts can help each component initially.

Fade support and test a new example. Repeating a prompted sequence does not establish independent use of the reasoning skill.

c04s04 Chapter 4: Distinguish cannot from cannot here.
Skill deficit: the required action is not yet learned. Performance condition: a factor affecting expression of a skill.

A person may know how to return a tool but lack access to the shelf. That is different from not knowing the procedure.

Before adding incentives, check instructions, tools, time and accessibility. A training solution does not fix every environmental barrier.

c04s05 Chapter 4: What does the evidence allow?
Learning procedures should be evaluated against the actual goal. Proxies such as speed or number of attempts can improve without better reasoning or safer performance.

Shaping is a learning principle, not a guarantee that every complex capability can be reduced to one fixed sequence.

c05s03 Chapter 5: Schedules describe arranged relations.
Continuous reinforcement: reinforcing every eligible response. Intermittent reinforcement: reinforcing some eligible responses. Reinforcement schedule: rule relating responses and consequences.

A schedule describes when a consequence is available in relation to action or time. It does not tell us whether the consequence actually reinforces for this person.

Learning history, task requirements and access also matter. State the arrangement before predicting an effect.

c05s03b Chapter 5: Ratio and interval concern different requirements.
Variable schedule: requirement varies around a pattern.

A ratio schedule uses a number of responses. An interval schedule makes reinforcement available after a time period, usually for the first eligible response.

Fixed and variable requirements can produce different patterns in controlled settings. They are not universal personality predictions or recipes for manipulating engagement.

c05s04 Chapter 5: Delay changes the learning problem.
Delay: time between action and consequence. Contingent feedback: information related to the action. Competing consequence: another outcome affecting the same behaviour.

A delayed outcome can be harder to connect with the relevant action. Clear informative feedback may help identify what was effective.

Real settings contain several consequences and verbal explanations. A schedule label alone rarely predicts a precise response rate outside a controlled arrangement.

c05s05 Chapter 5: What does the evidence allow?
Classic schedule findings come from defined experimental contingencies. Human behaviour also reflects instructions, goals, relationships and competing outcomes.

Do not use schedule research to justify addictive or deceptive design. The course evaluates claims rather than providing a manipulation playbook.

c06s03 Chapter 6: Extinction changes the predictive relation.
In classical extinction, the cue occurs without the expected outcome. In operant extinction, the action no longer produces the maintaining reinforcer.

Reduced responding does not necessarily mean the earlier learning was erased. New learning may be specific to a context and compete with the older relation.

c06s03b Chapter 6: Return has different forms.
Spontaneous recovery: response return after time has passed. Renewal: response return with a context change. Reinstatement: response return after relevant outcome exposure.

Time, a setting change or exposure to a relevant outcome can contribute to return of a response after extinction. These effects differ and require specific designs.

A returned response does not prove that change was impossible. It suggests that learning and retrieval conditions need to be examined.

c06s04 Chapter 6: Plan for changing conditions.
Generalization: responding across related cues or contexts. Maintenance: persistence of change over time.

A support may work in one room and fail elsewhere. Check different relevant settings and delayed outcomes rather than assuming one successful session is permanent.

Clinical exposure and relapse prevention require professional assessment and care. Do not create fear, withhold essential support or conduct exposure experiments as course practice.

c06s05 Chapter 6: What does the evidence allow?
Extinction and response-return effects are well-established distinctions, with outcomes depending on species, task and procedure. Translation to clinical care requires additional evidence.

The whistle example illustrates learning principles; it is not advice for treating anxiety or removing another person’s support.

c07s03 Chapter 7: Learning can extend beyond a trained cue.
Stimulus generalization: learned responding to related stimuli. Generalization gradient: response pattern across stimulus differences.

A learned response may occur to cues similar to the original. The relevant similarity can concern appearance, meaning or context.

Generalization is not guaranteed by a teacher’s intention. Check new cases rather than treating practice with one example as evidence of broad transfer.

c07s03b Chapter 7: Discrimination depends on a relation.
Stimulus discrimination: different responding to distinguishable cues. Discriminative stimulus: cue signalling an action-consequence relation.

A cue can signal when an action is likely to have a particular consequence. This differs from a cue eliciting a conditioned response.

To infer stimulus control, compare conditions while checking other differences. Colour, location, text size and instructions may all contribute.

c07s04 Chapter 7: Design transfer deliberately.
Multiple-exemplar training: practice with varied relevant examples. Overgeneralization: extending a rule beyond its valid range. Boundary condition: circumstance limiting a rule’s application.

Use varied examples that preserve the important rule and include cases where it does not apply. Ask the learner to explain the relevant feature.

The aim is useful discrimination and generalization, not identical responding everywhere. In safety-related tasks, professional standards and appropriate supervision remain essential.

c07s05 Chapter 7: What does the evidence allow?
Generalization and discrimination depend on learning history, attention and task structure. Evidence from one cue cannot establish universal stimulus control.

An everyday comparison with several simultaneous changes leaves causal alternatives unresolved.

c08s03 Chapter 8: Learning can use another person’s actions.

c08s03b Chapter 8: Attention and practice still matter.
Attention: selecting information for processing. Retention: keeping information available over time. Reproduction: ability to carry out the observed action.

A learner must notice relevant features and retain enough information to use them. Physical skills and opportunities affect whether the action can be reproduced.

Explain the purpose of each step, invite an attempt and provide feedback. Merely displaying a demonstration does not establish that the learner understood it.

c08s04 Chapter 8: Observed consequences shape expectations.
Vicarious reinforcement: effects of seeing another’s reinforcement. Outcome expectation: belief about likely consequences. Self-efficacy: belief in capability for a specified action.

Seeing another person succeed or receive a consequence can influence expectations and willingness to act. Believing a task is possible is distinct from already possessing the skill.

A relatable model and achievable practice may help, but social learning does not make learners passive copies. Prior knowledge and context shape interpretation.

c08s05 Chapter 8: What does the evidence allow?

A demonstration’s popularity is not evidence of transfer. Delayed unfamiliar performance provides a stronger check of the intended skill.

There is no universal number of days that creates every habit. Clinical compulsions, addiction and serious distress need appropriate care rather than a simplified habit checklist.

c09s05 Chapter 9: What does the evidence allow?

Self-directed routine experiments cannot establish treatment efficacy or explain every persistent behaviour.

c10s03 Chapter 10: A function is a hypothesis to test.
Antecedent: condition preceding an action. Consequence: event following an action. Functional assessment: examining relations between conditions and behaviour.

Map what happens before and after the action, then consider alternatives. A descriptive sequence does not by itself establish a causal function.

The locked shelf may be a barrier; a reminder may help; social expectations may matter. Use a safe, consensual comparison rather than assuming the first story is correct.

c10s03b Chapter 10: A change needs an evaluation design.
Single-case design: repeated measurement within a case under planned conditions. Multiple baseline: staggering introduction across cases or behaviours. Social validity: importance and acceptability of goals and procedures.

Repeated measurement can examine whether change follows an intervention. Replication across appropriately staggered introductions helps address alternative explanations.

Do not withdraw essential support to create a comparison. A meaningful outcome, participant preferences and harms are part of the evaluation, not optional extras.

c10s04 Chapter 10: Preserve agency in the application.
Consent: freely given informed agreement. Coercion: pressure undermining a free choice. Least restrictive approach: support that minimizes unnecessary constraint.

Discuss goals with the people affected and make access and skill support the first questions. Privacy and the right to decline matter.

Learning principles can improve teaching and self-directed routines. They do not justify manipulating partners, children or colleagues. Serious behavioural and clinical concerns need qualified support.

c10s05 Chapter 10: What does the evidence allow?
Behavioural effects depend on defined conditions, and practical interventions need evidence for both outcomes and acceptability. A count can improve while wellbeing or autonomy worsens.

This introduction is not professional behaviour-analysis training or clinical advice.
